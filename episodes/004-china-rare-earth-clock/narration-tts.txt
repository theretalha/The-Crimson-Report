[CHAPTER 0 — NINE DAYS]

In May 2025, a car factory in Chicago stopped building the Ford Explorer.

The reason was one small magnet, stuck waiting for a Chinese export licence.

Now China has set a new date. The tenth of January. <short pause>

And nine days earlier, America's got a deadline of its own.

Why are both governments watching those same nine days?

On the ninth of November, China's commerce ministry announced an extension.

Controls on rare earths, battery materials and processing equipment would stay suspended until the tenth of January.

That's two more months. Not two more years.

So what exactly is being rationed? Start with the magnet.


[CHAPTER 1 — THE SEVENTEEN METALS]

There's a family of seventeen metals called rare earths.

They aren't actually rare. They're just buried in small amounts, mixed together in the same rock.

It isn't easy to pull one metal away from the others. It takes rows of mixing tanks and many rounds of chemistry.

Two of them, neodymium and praseodymium, make the strongest magnets in everyday use.

A third, dysprosium, keeps those magnets from giving up when a motor's running hot. <short pause>

Inside an electric car, there's a magnet ring around the spinning rotor.

One data review averaged about one and a half kilograms of magnet in each electric car.

Fighter jets use them too. A widely quoted figure says each F-35 carries four hundred and seventeen kilograms of rare earth material.

But where does that magnet figure come from?

The research firm Adamas traces it to a single Pentagon report from 2012, never made public. <short pause>

So treat the number as a rough guide to the magnets inside, not a fact.

The International Energy Agency says China mined about sixty percent of the world's rare earths in 2024.

Yet it handled about ninety-one percent of the refining.

How does one country control more than its own mines produce?


[CHAPTER 2 — THE MIDDLE STOP]

Picture the supply chain as three stops. A mine. A separation plant. A magnet factory.

Plenty of countries have the ore, so the mine's the easy stop.

The middle stop is the separation plant, where mixed powder gets split apart one metal at a time. <short pause>

Rock goes in at one end. Jars of single-metal oxide come out the other.

Then the oxide's melted into alloy, pressed into blocks, and cut into magnets.

For the heavy metals like dysprosium, one industry estimate puts China near ninety-nine percent.

Lynas, an Australian company, began separating dysprosium in Malaysia in May 2025.

Fastmarkets reports it made eight tonnes of dysprosium and terbium in the first three months of 2026.

In 2024, China shipped about fourteen tonnes of those two to Japan alone. That's every single month.

Eight tonnes in three months. Fourteen tonnes a month. <short pause>

That's the gap that lets a licence form in Beijing stop a car factory in Chicago.

So how did that licence form actually work?


[CHAPTER 3 — THE LICENCE FORM]

In April 2025, China began requiring export licences for seven medium and heavy rare earths, and for magnets made from them.

Every shipment needed a stamp of approval from the commerce ministry in Beijing.

Ford's chief executive, Jim Farley, said the applications were being approved one at a time.

He called the supply hand to mouth. Ford had already shut one factory for about a week.

In June, China gave temporary licences to the magnet suppliers of the three biggest American carmakers, according to Reuters.

Suzuki halted its Swift line from the twenty-sixth of May, Reuters reported. Some European supplier plants shut too.

On the ninth of October 2025, Beijing went further. <short pause>

Law firms read the new rule this way. A product made anywhere would need a Chinese licence. The trigger was one part in a thousand of Chinese rare earths in its value.

China's commerce ministry said the scope was limited, and promised easier licensing.

Twenty-one days later, Trump and Xi met in Busan, South Korea.

China agreed to suspend that expansion for one year.

Washington paused its own rule on the affiliates of blacklisted companies.

It looked like a clean swap of tariffs for metals.

<long pause> But the pause covered only the new expansion. The April licences didn't go away.

So did the magnets actually start flowing again?


[CHAPTER 4 — TWO READOUTS]

In mid-May 2026, Trump flew to Beijing for a summit with Xi.

Afterwards, the White House said China had pledged to ease its limits on rare earth processing equipment.

China's commerce ministry then published its own summary of the summit. Rare earths weren't in it. <short pause>

Mining.com reported that Trump left Beijing with no rare earth deal confirmed.

Two readouts of the same meeting. One page mentions the rare earths. The other doesn't.

Last week, Trump and Xi met again, in Shenzhen. <short pause>

They agreed to draft a stability action plan, CNBC reported.

Beijing's summary left out rare earths again. The January deadline wasn't settled in public.

Analysts say shipments remain far below the levels from before the trade war.

So which readout should you believe? Use the customs data, which records what actually shipped.


[CHAPTER 5 — THE CUSTOMS DATA]

A year after Busan, analysts at the Center for Strategic and International Studies went through China's customs numbers.

In the ten months after the deal, total exports of these materials fell only two and a half percent. <short pause>

But combined shipments to the United States and Japan fell fifty-eight percent.

The total barely moved. The destinations did.

The monitoring group Global Trade Alert reads the same data differently. It argues China kept its word overall.

CSIS also found magnet exports rose thirteen percent after the truce. American imports of them fell eleven percent that same month.

Shipments to Europe jumped sixty percent.

So why would Beijing extend a pause, yet keep the squeeze on?

Japan shows the squeeze most clearly. On the sixth of January 2026, China barred dual-use exports to Japan's military.

Analysts compare it to 2010, when a sea dispute with Japan reportedly held up magnet shipments too.

Fastmarkets cites Chinese customs data showing no dysprosium oxide or terbium oxide reached Japan in 2026.

TrendForce reports that Japan's dysprosium imports fell eighty-two percent in the first half of the year.

China says the measures target military use and protect its national security.

Tokyo calls the new export controls unacceptable.

Now look at the price tags. Outside China, dysprosium oxide has climbed fast.

Benchmark Minerals says the European price rose one hundred and fifty percent in the third quarter alone.

S&P Global put the September average price in Europe at thirty-five hundred dollars a kilogram.

Platts assessed the price at forty-one hundred dollars in North America on the thirtieth of October. <short pause>

The same Benchmark review says Europe now pays about sixteen times the Chinese price for the same metal.

So why would a metal cost sixteen times more outside China?

Analysts point to two reasons. Chinese supply is tightly licensed, and new magnet makers are bidding for every kilogram left.


[CHAPTER 6 — THE AMERICAN DEADLINE]

Now the other end of those nine days. On the first of January 2027, a Pentagon rule takes effect.

Trade press describes it this way. Defense contractors can't deliver products with certain magnets mined or processed in China.

The rule also covers tungsten and tantalum. A contractor who can't certify its magnets could lose the contract.

According to industry reports, they've asked for a second delay. <short pause>

Their argument is simple. A supply chain from mine to magnet doesn't exist yet outside China, they say.

Can Washington build one in time?

In July 2025, the Pentagon took a four hundred million dollar stake in MP Materials.

MP runs the Mountain Pass mine in California.

The Pentagon also promised a price floor of one hundred and ten dollars a kilogram for neodymium and praseodymium. It's a ten-year promise.

One analysis said the Chinese price was under sixty at the time.

MP's big new magnet plant is due to open in 2028. At full size, it should make ten thousand tonnes a year. <short pause>

That's more than a year after the ban begins.

<long pause> That's why those nine days matter. America's ban starts before its own supply exists. China's pause ends nine days later.

But what will Beijing do on the tenth?


[CHAPTER 7 — THREE ENDINGS]

First ending. Another extension.

In September, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said he wasn't sure whether a bigger deal or another extension would follow.

Second ending. The pause simply ends, and the controls snap back. <short pause>

The October 2025 export controls would return, with their one-part-in-a-thousand reach.

Carmakers in Europe and Asia could need Chinese licences for cars they've already built.

A repeat of the Chicago car factory stoppage isn't out of the question.

Third ending. Nothing's announced. Licences just get slower.

That's close to what the customs data already shows about magnet shipments.

Analysts say that's why the mix looks odd. The pause keeps the talks alive. The licence form keeps the control.

Beijing's view is different. It calls export controls a normal tool for national security and for non-proliferation duties.

Washington and Tokyo see pressure that's applied one factory at a time.

Can anyone simply build around Chinese magnets?

Electrek reported in September that Tesla has built a new motor with no rare earths.

Lynas is building a bigger heavy rare earth plant in Malaysia. Its first products are expected within two years.

The research firm BMI says China's grip won't loosen for years.

None of those magnets will be rolling out by the tenth of January.

So watch three things before then. How fast licences get approved. The price of dysprosium. And whether a new leaders' meeting gets a date.

Our forecast: another extension, announced by officials in Beijing, and soon. <short pause>

But each extension buys weeks. The new magnet plants need years.

If you drive a new electric car, one of those magnets is probably already in your driveway.

<whispers> Nobody can say for sure which ending Beijing picks.
