A year after Busan, analysts at the Center for Strategic and International Studies went through China's customs numbers.

In the ten months after the deal, total exports of these materials fell only two and a half percent. <short pause>

But combined shipments to the United States and Japan fell fifty-eight percent.

The total barely moved. The destinations did.

The monitoring group Global Trade Alert reads the same data differently. It argues China kept its word overall.

CSIS also found magnet exports rose thirteen percent after the truce. American imports of them fell eleven percent that same month.

Shipments to Europe jumped sixty percent.

So why would Beijing extend a pause, yet keep the squeeze on?

Japan shows the squeeze most clearly. On the sixth of January 2026, China barred dual-use exports to Japan's military.

Analysts compare it to 2010, when a sea dispute with Japan reportedly held up magnet shipments too.

Fastmarkets cites Chinese customs data showing no dysprosium oxide or terbium oxide reached Japan in 2026.

TrendForce reports that Japan's dysprosium imports fell eighty-two percent in the first half of the year.

China says the measures target military use and protect its national security.

Tokyo calls the new export controls unacceptable.

Now look at the price tags. Outside China, dysprosium oxide has climbed fast.

Benchmark Minerals says the European price rose one hundred and fifty percent in the third quarter alone.

S&P Global put the September average price in Europe at thirty-five hundred dollars a kilogram.

Platts assessed the price at forty-one hundred dollars in North America on the thirtieth of October. <short pause>

The same Benchmark review says Europe now pays about sixteen times the Chinese price for the same metal.

So why would a metal cost sixteen times more outside China?

Analysts point to two reasons. Chinese supply is tightly licensed, and new magnet makers are bidding for every kilogram left.
