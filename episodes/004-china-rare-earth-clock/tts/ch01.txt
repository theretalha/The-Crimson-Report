There's a family of seventeen metals called rare earths.

They aren't actually rare. They're just buried in small amounts, mixed together in the same rock.

It isn't easy to pull one metal away from the others. It takes rows of mixing tanks and many rounds of chemistry.

Two of them, neodymium and praseodymium, make the strongest magnets in everyday use.

A third, dysprosium, keeps those magnets from giving up when a motor's running hot. <short pause>

Inside an electric car, there's a magnet ring around the spinning rotor.

One data review averaged about one and a half kilograms of magnet in each electric car.

Fighter jets use them too. A widely quoted figure says each F-35 carries four hundred and seventeen kilograms of rare earth material.

But where does that magnet figure come from?

The research firm Adamas traces it to a single Pentagon report from 2012, never made public. <short pause>

So treat the number as a rough guide to the magnets inside, not a fact.

The International Energy Agency says China mined about sixty percent of the world's rare earths in 2024.

Yet it handled about ninety-one percent of the refining.
